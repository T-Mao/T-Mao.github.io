DoneTodo App

A visual planner that puts planned time, actual time, and goals on one grid.

A time grid I wanted to carry with me

DoneTodo grew out of a planning system I had been using on paper since 2019. I designed my own journal templates and found a time grid especially useful for reviewing the day. Eventually I needed two views: what I intended to do, and what I actually did.

I published digital versions of those templates on the Goodnotes Marketplace in 2021. Later, I taught myself Swift and built an app around the same idea. DoneTodo brings Todo, Done, and Goal into one place: paint a plan, record the day, and compare the two.

I handled the product concept, interface, Swift and SwiftUI code, persistence, testing, and App Store release.

Get DoneTodo for iOS.


The interaction is the product

The main screen is a 24 × 6 grid, with each cell representing ten minutes. Users assign colors to activities and tap or drag to fill the grid. Planned and completed time have distinct visual treatments, so the same period can hold both an intention and a record.

A current-time marker anchors the grid in the day. Date swipes and a calendar make it possible to revisit earlier entries without turning the app into a folder of separate journals. Goals such as a minimum amount of study or a limit on phone time appear alongside the summaries.

This design asks less of the user than repeatedly entering a title, start time, and end time. The action resembles the paper habit that inspired it: mark the time directly.

Decisions beneath the grid

Give each cell two states

I modeled a cell with separate planned and completed activity references, along with its color information. Activity identifiers let the app associate the grid with editable task buttons. That separation matters when an activity is renamed, recolored, erased, or summarized later.

Make drawing recoverable

Painting can change many cells in one gesture. I added an undo/redo history, capturing grid snapshots before changes and limiting the stored history. An accidental stroke should be something the user can reverse, rather than a reason to stop using the grid.

Drawing and day navigation also share the same screen. I worked on their gesture handling so a movement could be interpreted in the intended context instead of unexpectedly changing the date.

Keep the record and the summaries connected

The app stores day schedules as JSON, including custom colors, and aggregates cell durations by activity. Statistics offer daily, weekly, monthly, yearly, and all-time views, with total and average values. Custom SwiftUI charts let a user inspect an activity without leaving the overall picture.

What this project taught me

DoneTodo made me think about the smallest units of an interaction. A ten-minute cell is simultaneously a drawing target, a piece of persisted data, a contribution to a statistic, and a state an undo operation must restore. Keeping those meanings consistent was more important than adding another screen.

The result is a released app built around a planning method I use myself. It reflects the kind of product work I enjoy most: take a familiar habit, find the friction, and work carefully through the details that make the digital version useful.
