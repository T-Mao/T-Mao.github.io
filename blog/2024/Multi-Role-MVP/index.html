The Work Around the Code

Published on September 27, 2024 , last updated on October 5, 2026

2024   ·   career   growth   flutter   ·   experience

My job at an early-stage company was to develop mobile apps. The work surrounding that job was much broader. I helped clarify requirements, coordinate development, support users, assist with hiring, and handle practical office tasks when someone needed to do them.

Those responsibilities gave me a closer view of how a product reaches people: through decisions, communication, support, and the less visible work that keeps a small company operating.

Requirements are a conversation before they are a ticket

A business request often arrived as an intended result rather than an implementable specification. “Make it easier for technicians to find jobs” sounds reasonable, but it leaves important questions unanswered. Which jobs qualify? What if the technician’s location is old? What should happen after another technician accepts an order?

Helping turn those requests into concrete tasks was part of my development work. I used documents and mind maps to make the sequence and dependencies easier to discuss. A useful requirement describes an observable behavior and the conditions around it.

The communication goes both ways. I needed to explain why a seemingly small UI change could require a backend change, another permission, or a new test scenario. That explanation works better when it describes the consequence for the product rather than the difficulty of the code.

Support makes the workflow real

Answering questions and reproducing reported problems put me closer to users’ expectations. Someone who says “payment didn’t work” may mean the card was declined, the screen stayed loading, the order did not update, or they were unsure whether to tap again.

Those are different issues with different fixes. I learned to ask what the user tried, what they expected, and what they actually saw. The device, app version, timing, and account state can be more useful than the first description of the error.

This perspective also changed how I think about polish. An ambiguous confirmation message can generate support work even when the server behaves correctly. Good error handling includes helping the person decide what to do next.

Hiring and onboarding sharpen explanation

I helped with candidate screening, interview scheduling, job postings, and onboarding. Those responsibilities made me pay closer attention to how technical expectations are communicated.

A task that feels obvious to the person who created it may depend on several undocumented assumptions. The same is true of a codebase. Explaining a project to someone new reveals where naming, setup instructions, or architectural boundaries are doing too little work.

Writing a useful job posting or helping a new colleague get started required the same care with assumptions that I wanted in a requirement or API contract.

Context switching has a cost

Moving between coding, coordination, support, and office logistics required adaptability and made protecting focused development time important.

A practice I recommend is leaving a brief restart note before changing tasks: the current hypothesis, the last checked result, and the next action. For a difficult bug, this can prevent the next work session from beginning with another hour of rediscovery.

The same principle applies to priorities. If an urgent request interrupts planned work, the schedule needs to reflect that decision. Recording the displaced work makes the tradeoff visible and keeps the plan realistic.

A wider view of ownership

Office logistics and administrative work gave me a fuller view of the constraints around a product. A delayed setup, an unanswered support question, or a missing decision could hold up the team as surely as a defect in the app.

Building the apps remained my central responsibility. Working with stakeholders, users, candidates, and new colleagues helped me make better development decisions because I understood more of the circumstances behind a request. I came away more comfortable asking a precise question, explaining a tradeoff, and carrying an issue through to a useful result.
