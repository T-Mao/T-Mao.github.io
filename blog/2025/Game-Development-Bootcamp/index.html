What a Game Development Course Taught Me About App Design

Published on July 19, 2025 , last updated on October 5, 2026

2025   ·   game-dev   product-management   indie-dev   cross-platform   professional-development   ·   career-growth

I love games, and I am curious about how they are made. That interest led me to complete How to Get Started in Game Development through LA County Library’s instructor-facilitated online learning program on July 19, 2025. The certificate records 24 hours of training.

I approached the introductory course as an app developer looking into another discipline. Games offered a useful perspective on feedback, interaction, production planning, and testing. These are the ideas I took from the course and the questions they raised for my app work.

Feedback is part of the interaction

A game must help the player understand what their action changed. A button press can affect position, resources, progress, or the rules of the next decision. If that relationship is unclear, the player cannot form a reliable mental model.

Apps need the same clarity. Did a task save? Did a payment begin processing? Was a letter scheduled? Animation or color can support that answer, but neither should be the only evidence.

The course made me pay closer attention to the loop between action and feedback. In an app, I would check how clearly the resulting state is visible, understandable, and accessible.

A prototype should answer a question

Interface design and early mockups are useful because they make an idea inspectable before a full implementation exists. The key is deciding what the prototype is meant to teach.

For a game, that might be whether a control scheme feels understandable. For a planning app, it might be whether the distinction between planned and completed work is clear. A focused prototype can answer that interaction question before the backend is complete.

A prototype’s limits should be visible to the people reviewing it. Placeholder state and hardcoded data can make an interaction easier to explore, while leaving persistence, errors, and deployment for later work. Keeping that scope clear makes the feedback more useful.

Choose incentives that fit the product

Games taught me to examine goals, challenges, and rewards more closely. Those mechanisms can help a user learn an interface or notice progress. They can also create unnecessary pressure when copied into the wrong product.

For a productivity app, I would favor clear progress and a neutral presentation of unfinished tasks. For a reflective app, I would make room for a thoughtful session that ends when the person is ready. The incentives should fit the user’s purpose.

That distinction matters to me as both a game enthusiast and someone who studies apps. An enjoyable interaction is valuable, but its success should be defined by the job the product helps someone do.

Production planning exposes dependencies

The course covered the roles and stages involved in making a game, including design, programming, assets, leadership, testing, and publishing. Seeing those responsibilities together was useful for independent app work, where one person may perform several of them.

A feature can depend on artwork, sound, written instructions, permissions, and release preparation as well as code. I would make those dependencies explicit in a small production plan rather than discover them during the final build.

Ownership and asset licensing also need attention. An image downloaded for a prototype may not be appropriate to ship. A practical asset record can preserve where it came from and how it may be used.

Testing includes the experience

A game can launch correctly and still have a confusing objective or an unreachable control. An app can be technically functional and still leave someone unsure what happened.

I would use early interaction reviews alongside technical tests, then check the experience under realistic constraints such as interrupted input, larger text, and poor connectivity where relevant. The aim is to discover where the person’s experience breaks down, even when the underlying feature works.

Course certificate

The course gave me another way to inspect an interaction: what can the person do, what changes, and how do they know? I find that perspective useful well beyond games, and it is one reason I keep learning outside my immediate work.
